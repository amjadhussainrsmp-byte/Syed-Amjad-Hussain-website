The Naqshbandiyya is one of the largest and most influential Sunni Sufi orders, named after Baha al-Din Naqshband of Bukhara (1318–1389). It is distinguished by silent remembrance of God, strict adherence to Islamic law, and the ideal of remaining spiritually inward while living an active life in society.

Origins

The Naqshbandi path grew out of the earlier Central Asian Khwajagan (“Masters”) tradition in Transoxiana, the region around modern Uzbekistan and Tajikistan. Early Khwajagan teachers developed many of the principles later associated with the Naqshbandi order. The order received its name from Baha al-Din Muhammad Naqshband, born near Bukhara in 1318. He did not create the entire tradition from nothing; rather, he consolidated and gave enduring form to the Khwajagan lineage.

Core teachings

Naqshbandi spirituality emphasizes inward purity without withdrawing from ordinary life. Its followers are encouraged to work, marry, raise families, and participate in society while keeping the heart focused on God. Its principal ideals include:

  • Silent dhikr: Remembrance of God in the heart rather than through loud, rhythmic chanting.
  • Shari‘a first: Spiritual practice must be grounded in Islamic law and Sunnah.
  • Solitude in society: The seeker maintains inner detachment while living and working among people.
  • Purposeful labor: Honest work and service are treated as part of spiritual discipline.
  • Guidance of a shaykh: The disciple follows a qualified master through the stages of the path.

The eleven principles

The Naqshbandi path is traditionally summarized in eleven principles. Eight were formulated by the early Khwajagan master Abd al-Khaliq Gujduwani, and three were added by later Naqshbandi masters. The best-known principles include:

  • Hush dar dam: Awareness while breathing—avoiding forgetfulness of God during inhalation and exhalation.
  • Nazar bar qadam: Watchfulness over one’s steps, symbolizing attention to every movement and action.
  • Nigah dasht: Guarding the heart against wandering thoughts.
  • Yad dasht: Continuous remembrance and awareness of the Divine presence.
  • Khalvat dar anjuman: “Solitude in the crowd”—being inwardly secluded while outwardly engaged with the world.

Practices

The central Naqshbandi practice is silent dhikr, performed individually and in congregational settings. Unlike some orders that use music, dance, or loud chanting, Naqshbandis generally prefer quiet, inward recitation. Important practices include:

  • Daily awrad: Personal litanies and prayers assigned by the shaykh.
  • Khatm al-Khwajagan: A weekly congregational remembrance, often held on Thursday night or Friday.
  • Muraqaba: Contemplative meditation and attention to the heart.
  • Rabita: Spiritual connection with the shaykh, used as a means of focusing the disciple’s heart.

The order generally avoids distinctive Sufi dress, loud musical sama, and prolonged withdrawal from society, reflecting its ideal of disciplined participation in ordinary life.

Spread and influence

From Bukhara and Central Asia, the Naqshbandiyya spread across Persia, Anatolia, the Ottoman lands, India, and later Southeast Asia. It became especially influential in the Ottoman Empire and in Muslim South Asia. In India, Khwaja Baqi Billah is credited with establishing the order in the late sixteenth century. His most famous disciple, Shaykh Ahmad Sirhindi (1564–1624), known as Mujaddid Alf-I Thani, led a major Naqshbandi reform movement that emphasized revival of Sunnah and resistance to religious syncretism.

Naqshbandiyya in South Asia

The Naqshbandiyya became a major force in Indian Muslim religious life and, for a period, was among the most widespread Sufi orders in the subcontinent. Its emphasis on Sunnah, scholarly learning, and social engagement made it influential among both elites and ordinary believers. Naqshbandi lineages later spread through Punjab, Delhi, Kashmir, and other regions, and the order remains active in India, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Turkey, Central Asia, and Southeast Asia.
