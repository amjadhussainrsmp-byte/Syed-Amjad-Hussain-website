The Qadiriyya is one of the oldest and most widespread Sufi orders in Sunni Islam, named after its founding figure, Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani (1077/78–1166), a Hanbali scholar and preacher based in Baghdad. It emphasizes lawful Islamic practice, humility, charity, inner struggle against the ego, and remembrance of God.

Origins

Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani was born in Gilan, in present-day Iran, and spent his teaching career in Baghdad. He studied Islamic law, became a celebrated preacher, and developed a reputation as a major spiritual master. Although he is called the founder of the Qadiriyya, the organized order largely took shape after his death. His sons and later disciples spread his teachings, establishing the order across North Africa, Central Asia, the Arabian Peninsula, India, and eventually Southeast Asia and parts of Eastern Europe.

Core teachings

Al-Jilani’s central achievement was to reconcile Sufi spirituality with the requirements of Islamic law. For him, the mystical path was not a departure from shari‘a but its inward completion. The Qadiri path stresses:

  • Piety and adherence to Islamic law: Worship, ethics, and spiritual discipline are grounded in normative Islamic practice.
  • Struggle against the ego: Al-Jilani described the spiritual struggle as a “holy war” against selfishness, worldliness, and the lower self.
  • Humility and moderation: Seekers are encouraged to avoid arrogance, excess, and attachment to worldly status.
  • Charity and humanitarian service: Philanthropy and generosity are treated as essential expressions of faith.
  • Submission to God’s will: The goal is sincere trust in God and conformity of the heart to divine guidance.

Spiritual practices

Qadiri devotional life centers on dhikr, the repeated remembrance of God, often through litanies, prayer formulas, and collective gatherings. The order is loosely organized, so regional communities have developed their own forms of dhikr and ritual prayers. Common practices include:

  • Dhikr: Vocal or silent remembrance of God, sometimes performed individually and sometimes collectively.
  • Wird and awrad: Daily litanies and prayer formulas, many associated with Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani.
  • Hadra: In some regions, communal devotional gatherings involving recitation and rhythmic movement.
  • Muraqaba: Meditation and contemplative attention directed toward God.
  • Ethical discipline: Service, restraint, humility, and care for others as practical spiritual training.

Organization and spread

Unlike some highly centralized Sufi orders, the Qadiriyya has generally been flexible and decentralized. Local shaykhs and lineages adapted the order to different languages, cultures, and social settings while maintaining devotion to Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani. This adaptability helped the order spread widely:

  • From Baghdad across the Fertile Crescent and North Africa.
  • Into Central Asia, Arabia, and India.
  • Later into West Africa, East Africa, Southeast Asia, and Eastern Europe.

Qadiriyya in South Asia

The Qadiriyya became a major Sufi presence in South Asia, including the Deccan, Punjab, and other regions. Qadiri teachers often interacted with local rulers and courts, though the order’s spiritual authority did not depend exclusively on political patronage. In India and Pakistan, the order contributed to shrine culture, devotional poetry, and the broader spread of Sufi teachings. Its accessibility to ordinary believers and emphasis on charity made it influential across many communities.

Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani’s legacy

Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani is honored across much of the Muslim world as Ghawth al-A‘zam (“the greatest helper” or “greatest saint”). His tomb in Baghdad remains an important pilgrimage destination, and his teachings continue to shape Qadiri lineages worldwide.
